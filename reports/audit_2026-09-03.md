# Audit, 3 September 2026

I re-derived every headline number in this repo from the committed parquet,
without reading the SQL that produced them, then compared what I got against
what the README, the memo and the Power BI guide say. This is what came back.

Short version: the analysis is sound and reproduces exactly. The prose around
it had four errors, one of which reversed the meaning of a sentence in the
memo. There is also a finding sitting in the data that nothing in the repo was
saying out loud.

## What reproduces

- Running `src/03_build_warehouse.sql` against the committed raw parquet in a
  fresh in-memory DuckDB reproduces `analysis_stress_penalty` and
  `analysis_ramp_stress_penalty` to the last decimal place, and both fact
  tables to the row.
- Recomputing MAPE, the stress split, skill against persistence, the
  balance-clean subset and the ramp lens in pandas, from the fact table alone,
  matches the published analysis tables with a maximum absolute difference of
  zero.
- Re-running the block bootstrap from scratch with a different seed and 4,000
  iterations instead of 2,000 moves no interval bound by more than 0.035 pp,
  and the significant/not-significant verdict is unchanged for all eight
  regions.
- 13 of 13 tests pass.

So the numbers are right. The problems were all in what got written about them.

## Errors found and fixed

**1. The memo's first finding contradicted itself.** It said only three
regions have a penalty distinguishable from zero, named NYIS as the one that
degrades, and then said "Everything else is noise: New York ISO has the
second-largest point estimate ... entirely consistent with no effect." The
cause was `worst = penalty.iloc[0]` picking the top row of a table sorted by
point estimate, which is NYIS, when the sentence needs the largest estimate
among the regions whose interval still contains zero. Fixed to draw from that
set, which is SWPP, and the rank is now derived rather than hardcoded.

**2. The SWPP interval was wrong everywhere it appeared.** README and the
Power BI guide both quoted [−1.00, +1.97]. `reports/bootstrap_intervals.csv`,
which is generated, says [−0.98, +1.93]. Three occurrences, corrected.

**3. Largest and best-pinned-down are different regions under the ramp lens,
and both the memo and the README said one region was both.** The memo picked
Southern Company by point estimate and called it "the largest and most tightly
bounded penalty in the study"; its interval, [+0.38, +4.56], is the widest in
the study. The README picked PJM and called it "the largest, tightest"; PJM's
+1.78 is second-largest. Both now name both regions and say which is which.
This one mattered: the memo's recommendation turned on it.

**4. CISO's balance-failure count was three different numbers in three
places** (6,430, 6,493, 6,552) for what was stated as the same quantity. The
gap is hours where interchange is not reported, which cannot fail the identity,
only go untested. `02_validate.py` now reports clean, dirty and unknown as
three separate columns instead of folding unknown into dirty, and the memo
counts against the hours the check can actually be run on: 6,396 of 17,304.

## Findings the repo was not making

**5. SWPP has one day of broken forecast data, and it drives a number the
narrative leans on.** From 01:00 on 2026-04-16 through midnight, SWPP's
reported day-ahead forecast runs between 5% and 34% of actual demand for 24
consecutive hours while its net generation tracks demand normally. The
validation script had an `implausible_D` check on demand but no equivalent on
the forecast, so nothing caught it. Removing that single day cuts SWPP's stress
penalty from +0.42 to +0.12 pp, which matters because SWPP is the example the
memo uses to argue that point estimates mislead. The argument survives, and is
arguably stronger, but the +0.42 was mostly one bad day. SWPP's negative skill
score barely moves (−0.77 to −0.74), so that separate finding does not depend
on it. Added as `implausible_DF` in `02_validate.py`, reported not corrected,
consistent with how the project treats the balance failures.

**6. Most of the error is one-directional, and nothing said so.** The warehouse
computes signed bias and the memo never mentions it. Seven of the eight regions
miss in the same direction in more than 65% of their hours. CISO's mean signed
error is −6.95 pp against an 8.36 MAPE, so 83% of its average absolute error is
a standing offset rather than noise; SWPP is the mirror image at +6.14 against
7.70. MISO runs high in 84% of hours, NYIS runs low in 84%. Only ERCOT, at 58%,
is near even.

This changes what a reader should do with the MAPE numbers. A noisy forecast
needs a better model. A forecast that misses the same way almost every hour
needs recalibrating, which is cheaper, and the direction tells you where the
exposure sits: an under-forecasting region buys short in the real-time market,
an over-forecasting one commits capacity it does not need. Two measures
(`Bias Share of Error`, `Hours Forecast Ran High (%)`) and a page in the Power
BI report now carry it, and the README says it in the findings list.

## Things that check out but are worth knowing

- **PJM's demand-stress improvement barely clears zero.** Its interval upper
  bound is −0.036 pp on the shipped seed and −0.050 on my independent one. It
  is called "significant" throughout, which is true, but it is the one verdict
  in the table that a different resampling could flip. SOCO sits just the other
  side at [−0.44, +0.03].
- **Timezone handling is correct.** Daily peak demand lands at hours 17 to 19
  local in every region, and CISO net load bottoms out at 14:00 and peaks at
  21:00, which is the duck curve in the right place. The double
  `AT TIME ZONE` in the warehouse does what its comment says.
- **The stress-hour definition is honest.** 5.01% of hours are flagged, against
  a 95th-percentile threshold, so the window function is doing what it claims.
- **"5-12% overlap"** rounds 4.6% and 11.6%. Fine, and the range is the point.
- **$734M** at $100/MWh reproduces exactly (7,338,982 MWh), as do the $293.6M
  and $1,467.8M ends of the band.

## One structural gap

`fact_fuel_hourly` had `ts_utc` and nothing else, so it could not join the
calendar or share an x axis with the demand fact. The Power BI guide worked
around it with a DAX calculated column derived from `ts_utc`, which would have
misaligned the two facts by a day for the third of the year where local
evening falls after UTC midnight. The warehouse now carries `ts_local` and a
local-derived `date_key` on the fuel table. Analysis outputs are unchanged;
verified by rebuild.
